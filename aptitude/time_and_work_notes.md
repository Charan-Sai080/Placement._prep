# Aptitude Masterclass: Time & Work

The goal of this document is to bypass messy fractions entirely. By understanding how to manipulate "Units of Work", you can solve these problems with simple whole-number arithmetic.

## 1. The Core Intuition
If you can build 1 wall in 5 days, how much do you build in 1 day?
Exactly **1/5th** of the wall. 

This is the golden rule of Time and Work: 
**You can never add "Days" together.** (If A takes 5 days and B takes 10 days, together they don't take 15 days!).
**But you CAN add "Work done per day" together.**

---

## 2. The LCM (Total Units) Method — The Secret Weapon
Working with fractions like 1/5 and 1/10 is slow and error-prone. Instead, we can invent an arbitrary number for the "Total Work" to force all our math to use clean, whole numbers.

**The Method:**
1. Find the LCM (Least Common Multiple) of all the days given in the problem. This is your "Total Units of Work".
2. Divide the Total Units by each person's days to find their **Daily Speed**.
3. Add or subtract the Daily Speeds based on who is working together.

*Example: A takes 10 days, B takes 15 days. How long together?*
1. **Find LCM:** The LCM of 10 and 15 is 30. Let's pretend the job is to eat **30 chocolates**.
2. **Find Daily Speed:**
   - A eats 3 chocolates/day (30/10)
   - B eats 2 chocolates/day (30/15)
3. **Combine:** Together they eat $3 + 2 = \mathbf{5 \text{ chocolates/day}}$.
4. **Solve:** To eat all 30 chocolates, it takes $30 / 5 = \mathbf{6 \text{ days}}$.

---

## 3. The "Wages" Concept
Money is NEVER divided by how many days someone worked.
**Money is divided based on the RATIO OF WORK DONE.**

If everyone works for the exact same amount of time, the money is split based on their **Daily Speed**. 
*   If A's speed is 8 units/day and C's speed is 2 units/day... A gets 4 times as much money as C.
*   If C completes 2 units out of a daily total of 16 units, C is doing 2/16 (or **1/8th**) of the work. Therefore, C gets exactly 1/8th of the total money.

---

## 4. The "Alternating/Intermittent Days" Concept
When people take turns (e.g., "A works day 1, B works day 2"), do not calculate day by day! It takes too long.

Instead, group them into a **"Cycle"**. 
1. Determine how many days make up one repeating Cycle (e.g., 2 days).
2. Calculate the total units completed in that single Cycle.
3. Divide the Total Units by the Cycle Units to find out how many Cycles you need.
4. Multiply the number of Cycles by the number of days in a Cycle to get the final answer.
